Gold has a long cultural history in Indian households. Silver less so. As financial assets, both behave differently from equity and bonds, and that difference is the main reason to consider a small allocation.

## What precious metals do in a portfolio

Gold has tended to hold value, or rise, in some periods when equity markets were under stress or when the rupee weakened. It does not produce income, and over long stretches it can go nowhere. Its usefulness is less about return and more about **behaving differently** from the rest of the portfolio.

Silver is more volatile than gold, partly because a large share of demand is industrial. It can rise faster in good periods and fall harder in poor ones.

## Ways to hold them

- **Gold and silver ETFs** track the domestic price and trade on the exchange; you need a demat account.
- **Gold and silver funds of funds** invest in those ETFs and can be bought like any mutual fund, including by SIP.
- **Multi-asset allocation funds** hold equity, debt and at least 10% in another asset class, often gold or silver, and rebalance between them within the fund.

## How much is enough?

For most diversified portfolios, precious metals are a supporting role, often a single-digit percentage of the total, decided as part of the overall allocation rather than as a bet on prices. The right figure depends on the rest of your holdings and your goals.

## A word of caution

Recent strong returns from any asset make it tempting to add more. The case for a metal allocation is diversification, not momentum. If the reason to buy is "it has done well lately", it is worth pausing.
