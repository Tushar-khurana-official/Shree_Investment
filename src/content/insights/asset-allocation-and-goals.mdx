Most investors spend their energy choosing funds. The decision that does far more to shape the result is made before any fund is chosen: how much goes into equity, how much into debt, and how much, if any, into gold. That split is your **asset allocation**.

## Why the split matters more than the scheme

Two well-run large-cap funds will usually end a decade within a few percentage points of each other. A portfolio that is 80% equity and one that is 30% equity will not. One can fall by a third in a bad year; the other may barely notice. Over a long horizon, the gap in outcomes between those two portfolios is far larger than the gap between two reasonable funds in the same category.

## Tie the allocation to the date, not the mood

The right mix depends on when you need the money:

- **Ten years or more away** (retirement, a young child's degree): mostly equity, because there is time to recover from falls.
- **Three to seven years away**: a balance, moving gradually towards debt as the date approaches.
- **Under three years away** (a house down payment, next year's fees): little or no equity. A fall at the wrong moment cannot be waited out.

This is why a single "risk profile" for a whole family rarely works. The same household can have money that should be 90% equity and money that should be 0%.

## Agree the rebalancing rule in advance

Markets will push your mix away from the plan. After a strong year in equity, a 60:40 portfolio might become 70:30. A rebalancing rule, for example "rebalance when equity is more than five percentage points away from target", decides in advance what you will do, so the decision is not made in the middle of euphoria or panic.

> The allocation is the plan. The schemes are just how the plan is carried out.

## What this means in practice

When we build a plan, each goal gets its own allocation and its own glide path towards its date. Schemes are chosen afterwards, to fill each slot, and reviewed against that job, not against last year's league table.
